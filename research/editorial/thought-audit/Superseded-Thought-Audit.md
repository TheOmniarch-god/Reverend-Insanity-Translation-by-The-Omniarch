## Scope and verdict

The problem is not that all third-person representations of thought are wrong. Free indirect thought is legitimate. The actionable problems are abrupt, unmarked changes of grammatical viewpoint and passages where ordinary dialogue formatting makes a private thought look spoken.

## Priority findings

### 1. Chapter 3, p26–p27: clear mixed-person passage

URL: https://ri.theomniarch.com.ng/chapter-3#p26

Live sequence:

> So what if he was his own younger brother? ... Devoid of love and loyalty, she is nothing more than flesh. Take her as one of my concubines? She’s not worthy. So what if they were his uncle and aunt ... Why exhaust his ingenuity and waste his energy ...

Then:

> As long as they do not block my path, they can piss off. I wouldn’t even deign to step on them.

The thinker is Fang Yuan throughout. English moves from third-person/past through first-person/present, back to third-person, then first-person again without signalling the changes. “He was his own younger brother” is also awkward: the two pronouns refer to different people, despite the reflexive-looking construction.

The Chinese is more fluid, often omitting possessives, and only explicitly introduces 我 in the final declaration. That does not oblige English to reproduce a confusing pronoun pattern.

**Recommendation:** keep the reflection consistently direct, or consistently free indirect. Do not mix them accidentally.

A direct-thought model:

> *So what if he is my younger brother? Without any affection between us, he is no different from an outsider. I can let him go. And what if Shen Cui is beautiful? Without love or loyalty, she is nothing more than flesh. Take her as one of my concubines? She is not worthy. As for my uncle and aunt, the clan leader and the elders, they are merely passing through my life. Why waste my ingenuity and energy putting these passersby in their place?*
>
> *As long as they do not block my path, they can piss off. I would not even deign to step on them.*

This is a viewpoint-repair model, not a final stylistic replacement for the entire passage.

### 2. Chapter 8, p7a: direct and indirect thought joined without a clear boundary

URL: https://ri.theomniarch.com.ng/chapter-8#p7a

Live:

> To be safe, I should choose a Moonlight Gu, Fang Yuan thought. He had hoped ... Who knew when he would find them ...

The first clause is first-person direct thought with a tag; the next sentences are third-person narrated reflection. This is intelligible, but the shift is avoidably awkward. The Chinese gives the reasoning and decision as one quoted internal passage, with the decision at the end:

> ...保险起见，还是选一只月光蛊吧。

**Recommendation:** either keep the entire internal passage direct or render the entire sequence indirectly. Preserve reason before conclusion when possible.

Direct-thought model, following the separate narrator’s novice rule:

> *I had hoped to obtain the Flower Wine Monk’s Liquor Worm and refine it as my vital Gu. But I still have no clue where his remains lie. Who knows when I will find them, or whether someone else will find them first? To be safe, I should choose a Moonlight Gu.*

Alternative final decision in indirect narration:

> To be safe, Fang Yuan decided to choose a Moonlight Gu.

### 3. Chapter 8, p29: the case already discussed

URL: https://ri.theomniarch.com.ng/chapter-8#p29

Chinese:

> “这就是哥哥以前的感觉啊，真是美妙而又痛苦！”

Live:

> So this was how his brother used to feel: wonderful, and painful too!

The Chinese explicitly quotes Fang Zheng’s thought. The English deliberately or inadvertently converts it to free indirect thought. The English is not wrong merely for using third person, but it no longer preserves the source’s direct presentation.

If retaining direct thought:

> *So this is how my brother used to feel. Wonderful, yet painful!*

The following narratorial sentence can still name Fang Yuan. The following question, which is unquoted in Chinese, can legitimately remain free indirect:

> How had his brother dealt with all those comments and all that attention?

Alternatively, mark that question as direct too and use “my brother”. What matters is a readable, intentional boundary, not matching every source quotation mark mechanically.

### 4. Chapter 6, p23: an unmarked first-person intrusion

URL: https://ri.theomniarch.com.ng/chapter-6#p23

Live:

> And now that hope lay within his own body ... What is the disappointment of others to me? What matters is that one keeps hope in one’s own heart!

This moves from third-person narration into first-person thought and then a general maxim. The source itself becomes first-person at 别人的失望与我何干, so the first-person thought is justified. The English should signal its entry rather than change pronouns mechanically.

Source-verified wording:

> 别人因此而失望，那就失望吧，又能如何呢？
> 别人的失望与我何干？重要的是自己心存希望！

Proposed treatment:

> If others were disappointed, let them be. What of it?
>
> *What does their disappointment have to do with me? What matters is that I hold hope in my own heart!*

The first-person “me” is explicitly supported by 我. The earlier proposal’s “still” has been removed because it is not explicit in the source sentence. These Chinese lines are unquoted interior reflection. Italics above illustrate an optional English thought-formatting convention, not source formatting and not a spoken declaration.

Alternatively, keep the whole passage free indirect: “What did their disappointment have to do with him? What mattered was that he himself held hope in his heart.” That is an editorial narrative choice, not a required correction of meaning.

### 5. Chapter 8, p34 and p40: private thoughts look like spoken dialogue

URL: https://ri.theomniarch.com.ng/chapter-8#p34

Live standalone quotations:

> “I am exceptional. That alone is the truth!”

and:

> “What's wrong with me? Don't I even have the courage to look my brother in the eye? I have changed! I have completely changed!”

Both are Fang Zheng’s thoughts. The Chinese presents the first amid his private self-justification. The second follows his anger at himself and is followed by 这样想着, “thinking this”. Neither is introduced as speech.

The first-person pronouns are appropriate. The issue is ordinary dialogue quotation marks without a thought cue, especially when the brothers are physically together in the later scene.

**Recommendation:** use established thought formatting or a minimal tag such as “he told himself”. Do not make Fang Zheng say these aloud.

### 6. Chapter 5, p23: the inward-counting cue has been lost

URL: https://ri.theomniarch.com.ng/chapter-5#p23

Live puts the threshold explanation and his count in one standalone quotation, ending:

> ...So far, I have taken twenty-three steps. Twenty-four… twenty-five… twenty-six… twenty-seven.

The source explicitly says 方源心中默默地数着, “Fang Yuan counted silently in his mind”, for the count.

**Recommendation:** restore the internal cue or use thought formatting. This is more than a pronoun preference: the source specifies that the counting is silent.

Example:

> *Twenty-four… twenty-five… twenty-six… twenty-seven,* Fang Yuan counted silently.

Keep the preceding threshold exposition in whichever mode the full passage’s chosen treatment requires.

## Additional clarity checks

### Chapter 2, p21: spoken direct address is too literal

Live Fang Zheng says:

> “Seeing brother’s window wasn’t closed, I wanted to slip in quietly and shut it.”

He is speaking to Fang Yuan, not about an absent brother. Chinese uses 哥哥 naturally in direct address and possessive expressions; English normally uses “your” here.

Suggested:

> “I saw that your window was open, so I wanted to slip in quietly and close it.”

Keep “Brother” as a vocative elsewhere if desired. This is not the same as changing narratorial “his brother” to “my brother”.

### Chapter 1, p36; Chapter 4, p6/p28; Chapter 7, p26/p45

These are quoted internal passages whose context establishes or strongly indicates thought, but a reader may initially read the quotation marks as spoken dialogue. Do not classify them as wrong-person errors. A consistent thought-formatting convention or occasional “he thought” would distinguish them more clearly.

Chapter 1 p36 is the Clan Leader’s private calculation. Chapter 4 p6 is Fang Yuan’s knowledge of the flowers, not an explanation addressed to the other students. Chapter 4 p28 is his private analysis of the deception. Chapter 7 p26 continues the Academy Elder’s internal judgement; p45 continues Fang Yuan’s private recollection.

### Chapter 2, p33: a useful internal/external contrast

The source moves from Fang Yuan’s thought about revenge and the demonic path into an explicitly spoken dismissal. English preserves “Thinking to this point” and “he said”, but using identical quotation styling for both can blur the transition on first reading.

Keep the distinction: the philosophical sentence is thought; “You can go” is speech. Do not relabel the whole paragraph as either thought or dialogue.

## Passages not to falsely flag

- **Chapter 2, p21–p22:** Fang Zheng’s feelings of inferiority are presented through narration and free indirect thought. “His brother” is appropriate. Do not turn every sentence into a first-person quotation.
- **Chapter 2, p27:** “And as for his own aptitude…?” belongs to Fang Yuan’s focalised reflection. Its third-person construction is defensible.
- **Chapter 6, p11:** “His own elder brother was only of Grade C?” is a legitimate unquoted rendering of Fang Zheng’s incredulity.
- **Chapter 8, p31–p33:** the reflection on his brother’s decline is valid as narrated interiority/free indirect thought. No blanket pronoun replacement is warranted.
- **Chapter 3, p8:** Fang Zheng’s fantasy about Shen Cui is already marked as a thought by “entertained the thought”.
- **Chapter 5, p4:** “murmured inwardly” correctly identifies the Hope Gu sentence as internal.
- **Chapter 7, p25:** “in his heart” establishes the elder’s internal reaction, even though the wording could be smoother.

## Working rule for the next pass

1. Identify who owns the thought before editing any pronoun.
2. Distinguish explicitly spoken language from internal language using context and speech/thought verbs, not quotation marks alone.
3. Choose direct thought or free indirect thought deliberately.
4. Direct thought normally uses the thinker’s “I/my”; free indirect thought normally uses narrator-compatible third person and tense.
5. Do not shift between those modes inside one sentence or paragraph without a clear reason or boundary.
6. Do not add “said”, “muttered”, or “whispered” to a private thought merely to solve a punctuation problem.
7. Chinese 自己 and kinship terms do not map mechanically onto a single English pronoun.

**Priority order:** Chapter 3 p26–p27; Chapter 8 p7a and p29; Chapter 6 p23; Chapter 8 p34/p40; Chapter 5’s silent count. Then standardise the remaining thought cues without converting legitimate free indirect narration wholesale.
